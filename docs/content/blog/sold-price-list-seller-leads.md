# How to generate seller leads with a sold-price list

Most seller ads ask for too much, too soon.

“What is your home worth?” sounds useful, but it also signals an appraisal call. An owner who is only beginning to think about selling may not be ready for that conversation. They are often ready for something smaller: seeing what comparable homes nearby have actually sold for.

That makes a focused sold-price list a strong real estate lead magnet. It gives homeowners useful local information without asking them to declare that they are selling.

The idea is supported by how sellers already behave. In its [seller-leads guidance](https://customer.realestate.com.au/resource-hub/agency-marketplace/how-to-agency-marketplace/articles/seller-leads-best-practice/), realestate.com.au reports that 75% of sellers look at sold-property listings to help them find an agent. A sold-price campaign meets that behaviour with a clear offer instead of another generic promise about service.

The campaign is simple:

1. Choose one suburb and one recognisable property type.
2. Build a current list from data and images you are allowed to use.
3. Promote the list with a Meta instant form.
4. Deliver it immediately.
5. Follow up with context, consent and a useful next step.

The list is the offer. The ad only introduces it.

## Why the offer works

A suburb market report is broad. A list of recent sales for “single-storey homes on full blocks in Dianella” feels personal to someone who owns exactly that kind of property.

That specificity does two jobs.

First, it makes the value obvious. The owner can tell in a second whether the list is relevant to their home.

Second, it helps qualify the response. The form may still attract buyers, investors and curious neighbours, but the offer gives the right homeowner a stronger reason to submit than a general market update would.

This is not a shortcut to an immediate listing. It is an early-intent campaign. Its job is to identify local owners who care enough about recent comparable sales to ask for the detail.

## The Blockwise LIST framework

Use four checks before you spend anything.

| Check | What to decide | Good example | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| **L: Localise** | Pick one suburb, or two adjoining suburbs if sales volume is thin | “Recent sales in Scarborough” | “Perth property update” |
| **I: Isolate** | Choose one property characteristic owners recognise | “Villas in Yokine” | Six filters and a spreadsheet-sized audience |
| **S: Source** | Use current, disclosed results and media you are licensed to publish | Sale date, disclosed price, address and approved photo | Withheld prices, stale results or copied portal images without usage rights |
| **T: Tie to follow-up** | Connect delivery, consent, lead ownership and the next useful message | Instant delivery, named owner, clear opt-out | Leads sitting in a download or receiving an unexplained sales sequence |

If one of these checks is weak, fix it before building the ad.

## 1. Pick the patch you want to win

Start with the suburb where you want more listings, not simply the area with the largest number of recent transactions.

One suburb is usually enough. If it has too few disclosed sales, extend the reporting period before expanding the geography. Six months of relevant local results will usually make a more coherent offer than three months of scattered sales across five suburbs.

The campaign also needs enough budget and audience to deliver, so treat the boundary as a working hypothesis. If the audience is too small for stable delivery, add an adjoining suburb that shares the same housing stock. Do not turn a local offer into a citywide report to rescue the audience size.

## 2. Choose one property type

The property filter should be visible and easy for an owner to recognise. Useful options include:

- single-storey homes on full blocks
- villas in a particular pocket
- homes with side access
- corner blocks
- homes with pools
- duplex or subdivision-potential sites, where that description is accurate

Choose one. The point is not to build a perfect valuation model. It is to create a list that makes the right owner think, “These are homes like mine.”

Avoid filters that depend on assumptions you cannot substantiate. “Development potential,” for example, may require planning checks that a listing photo cannot provide. If the label needs a footnote to remain accurate, choose a simpler property type.

## 3. Build a list worth requesting

The lead magnet must keep the promise made by the ad. At minimum, each result should include:

- property address
- disclosed sale price
- sale date
- a short factual property description
- an image you have permission to use
- the date the list was last updated

Use agency records, an authorised data provider, or another source whose terms allow you to republish the information. Do not assume that because a result or image is visible on a portal, it is free to copy into your own landing page or advertising.

Leave withheld prices out. A short list of verified, disclosed results is more useful than a longer list padded with missing information.

Host the list on a fast mobile page. Put the update date near the title, keep the results scannable, and include a plain explanation of the area, property type and reporting period. The page should be useful even if the visitor never books an appraisal.

## 4. Set up the Meta campaign

Meta changes labels and defaults regularly, so follow the current options shown in your ad account. The basic setup is:

- **Objective:** Leads
- **Special Ad Category:** Housing, when the campaign relates to housing
- **Conversion location:** Instant form
- **Performance goal:** Maximise number of leads for a simple first test, or maximise conversion leads when your CRM and Conversions API are set up to return qualified outcomes
- **Audience:** The chosen suburb or local service area, without unnecessary interest stacking
- **Placements:** Advantage+ placements unless you have evidence for a specific exclusion
- **Schedule:** A fixed test window with a named review date

Advantage+ is now the default setup for eligible leads campaigns. Treat detailed audience inputs as suggestions and add controls only where the campaign or your compliance obligations genuinely require them.

### Set a budget that creates a clean test

A$25 to A$30 a day is a reasonable planning example for a small local test, not a universal benchmark. At that level, a 14-day test costs A$350 to A$420 before any tax or account-specific charges.

Use a lifetime budget if you need a hard campaign cap. If you use a daily budget, remember that Meta describes it as an average: it may spend up to 75% above the daily amount on a particular day, while keeping weekly spend within seven times the daily budget.

Do not split a small budget across several property types at once. Run one clear offer, record the result, then test the next meaningful change.

## 5. Make the creative explain the offer

Start with a strong image of a local home that matches the property type. Use an agency-owned or appropriately licensed image, and make sure any claim about the property is accurate.

A static image is a sensible first format because the offer is simple, but it is not a rule. The right question is whether someone can understand the list while scrolling. Preview the creative across the placements Meta will use and adapt the crop or layout where needed.

Keep the ad focused on the information, not the agent.

**Primary text**

> See the latest disclosed sale prices for single-storey homes in [Suburb]. The list covers the past [period], includes photos and sale dates, and is updated [frequency]. If you own a similar home and want a clearer view of the local market, request the list below.

**Headline**

> Recent single-storey sales in [Suburb]

**Call to action**

> Learn more

Avoid implying that the list is a valuation. Comparable sales provide market context; they do not establish what a specific property will sell for.

## 6. Keep the form short and explicit

An instant form reduces friction because Meta can prefill contact details. That convenience makes the review step important: people need a chance to check that their email address and mobile number are current.

Ask only for information you will use:

- full name
- email address
- mobile number, if phone follow-up is part of the disclosed process
- one qualifying question, such as “Do you own a home of this type in [Suburb]?”

Link to the agency privacy policy and explain what happens after submission. If available in your account, use the higher-intent form option so the person reviews their details before submitting.

Keep consent clear. Delivering the requested list is one action; adding someone to ongoing marketing is another. If you plan to send monthly updates, ask for express consent in plain language and keep a record of when and how it was given.

For example:

> Yes, send me the sold-price list and occasional [Suburb] property updates from [Agency]. I can unsubscribe at any time.

[Australian commercial email and SMS rules](https://www.acma.gov.au/avoid-sending-spam) require consent, accurate sender identification and a working unsubscribe method. An unsubscribe request must be honoured within five working days.

## 7. Deliver first, then start the conversation

The thank-you screen and first email should link directly to the list. Do not make the person wait for an agent to call before receiving what they requested.

Then assign the lead to a named person and follow up while the request is still familiar. Speed matters operationally, but there is no need to invent a universal five-minute rule. A reliable same-day response beats an “instant” automation that sends the wrong message.

A useful first message sounds like this:

> Hi Sarah, Steve from [Agency]. Here is the [Suburb] sold-price list you requested: [link]. The results cover [property type] sold since [date]. If you would like, I can also point out the two sales most comparable to your place. Reply STOP if you do not want messages from us.

The message delivers value, identifies the sender and offers a logical next step. It does not pretend the owner requested an appraisal.

For leads who consented to updates, the ongoing sequence can stay simple:

1. **Immediately:** deliver the list.
2. **Same business day:** offer help interpreting the closest comparable sales.
3. **Two or three days later:** share one relevant local observation or answer a common question.
4. **When the list updates:** send the new results with an opt-out in every commercial message.

Stop when someone opts out. Record replies and qualification consistently so the campaign is judged on useful conversations, not form volume alone.

## How to judge the 14-day test

Cost per lead is only one measure. Review the entire path.

| Measure | What it tells you | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Form completion rate | Whether the offer and form are easy to understand | Are people abandoning a form that asks too much? |
| Cost per lead | What Meta charges for a submitted form | Is the cost sustainable for this test, without assuming every lead is equal? |
| Owner-qualified rate | How many leads match the suburb and property type | Did the offer attract the people it named? |
| Contactable rate | Whether the details work and people respond | Are the form review and delivery steps doing their job? |
| Conversation rate | How many leads enter a useful property discussion | Does the follow-up continue the promise of the ad? |
| Appraisal and listing outcomes | The commercial result over time | Which leads progressed, and how long did that take? |

Do not edit the campaign every day. Let the test run long enough to produce a readable result, while still checking for broken links, rejected ads, lead-delivery failures and inappropriate spend.

At the review date, make one decision:

- **Continue** if the campaign is attracting the intended owners and the follow-up is working.
- **Revise** if the offer is relevant but the creative, form or delivery path is weak.
- **Stop** if the audience is wrong, the data cannot be maintained, or the economics do not support another test.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a sold-price list the same as a property valuation?

No. It shows disclosed results for a defined group of properties. It can help an owner understand recent local evidence, but it does not account for every feature, condition, legal constraint or market factor affecting their home.

### Should the ad show the sold prices?

The ad can show a verified example if you have the right to use it, but it does not need to reveal the full list. The offer should make clear that the requested resource contains the disclosed results.

### Should I use an instant form or a website form?

An instant form is a sensible first test when you want a fast mobile submission and straightforward contact fields. A website form may suit a higher-consideration flow that requires more explanation or an on-site action. Meta also supports using both approaches, but a small first test is easier to read when the path stays simple.

### How often should I update the list?

Choose a frequency you can maintain. Monthly is useful in an active suburb, while a slower market may need a longer interval. Put the last-updated date on the page and do not promise a schedule the agency cannot keep.
